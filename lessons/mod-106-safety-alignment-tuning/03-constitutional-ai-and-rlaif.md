# Constitutional AI and RLAIF Self-Critique Loops

**Estimated read:** 45 minutes.
**You should be able to, by the end:** describe the two-stage Constitutional AI recipe (SL-CAI + RL-CAI) and what problem each stage solves, write a small "constitution" that a critique-and-revise loop can consume, implement a critique-and-revise pipeline against an open judge model, and understand when RLAIF earns its complexity relative to plain preference data.

## Why "AI feedback" at all

The dataset chapter (chapter 2) assumed the responses in your safety training set came from *humans*: a labeler produced the safe response, a labeler judged which of two responses was safer. That was the InstructGPT / HH-RLHF recipe from 2021–2022.

Two obvious problems with the human-labeled path:

1. **Cost and throughput.** A skilled labeler produces on the order of tens to a few hundred safety-labeled rows per hour. A production safety dataset wants tens of thousands of rows across your full taxonomy, refreshed as the model changes. Human labor scales linearly with the dataset.
2. **Consistency and policy fidelity.** Labelers disagree. Anthropic's HH-RLHF paper reports annotator agreement in the low-to-mid 70s%; that ~25% disagreement is training noise. Different labelers apply different implicit policies unless they receive extensive per-task guidance.

Constitutional AI (CAI), introduced by Bai et al. (2022, Anthropic — "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback"), replaces both bottlenecks with a large language model, following an *explicit written policy* — the "constitution."

The one-line summary: **a model rewrites its own outputs and judges its own outputs against a written set of principles.** The rewrites become SFT training data; the judgments become preference training data. A team writes principles once; the model applies them millions of times.

The related term **RLAIF** — Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback — refers specifically to the second half of the recipe (Lee et al., 2023, "RLAIF: Scaling Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback with AI Feedback"), where the *preference labels* used to train the reward model or DPO come from an LLM judge rather than a human. CAI includes RLAIF as its second stage; the two names are often used interchangeably in casual writing, but CAI ⊃ RLAIF strictly.

## The two-stage CAI recipe

Bai et al.'s Constitutional AI paper describes a two-stage pipeline. Modern safety-tuning setups (Anthropic's own Claude, several open reproductions, and the "self-alignment" family — Sun et al., 2023, "SELF-ALIGN: Principle-Driven Self-Alignment of Language Models from Scratch with Minimal Human Supervision") follow the same shape.

```
Stage 1: SL-CAI (supervised)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Red-team prompt x
       │
       ▼
  Initial response y₀ ←── generated by the (currently unsafe) model
       │
       ▼
  Critique step:
    "Which principle from the constitution does y₀ violate?"
    → critique c
       │
       ▼
  Revision step:
    "Rewrite y₀ so it does not violate that principle."
    → revised response y₁
       │
       ▼
  New SFT row: (x, y₁)   ← this is what you train on
       │
       ▼
  Fine-tune the base with SFT on many such (x, y₁) rows
       │
       ▼
  Result: SL-CAI model


Stage 2: RL-CAI (preference / RL)
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  Red-team prompt x
       │
       ▼
  Sample two responses y_A, y_B ←── from the SL-CAI model
       │
       ▼
  AI-judge step:
    "According to the constitution, which of y_A, y_B is
    less harmful? Consider principle P1, P2, ..."
    → judgment: chosen = y_A, rejected = y_B
       │
       ▼
  Preference pair (x, y_A, y_B) ← train DPO / reward-model + PPO here
       │
       ▼
  Result: RL-CAI model
```

Two things to notice about the structure:

- **Stage 1 fixes the response distribution.** The SL-CAI model produces safer responses than the base model, in your target voice, on any prompt in the coverage set. This is a substitute for hand-labeling a safe-completion SFT set (chapter 2's SFT-side mix).
- **Stage 2 fixes the *preference*.** The RL-CAI model has been shown pairwise which responses are safer under the constitution, and learned to prefer them. This is a substitute for hand-labeling a safety-preference set (chapter 2's preference-side mix).

You can run stage 1 without stage 2 (and get an SFT-only safety improvement); running stage 2 without stage 1 works but tends to be less effective because the pairs are sampled from a model whose response distribution is not yet safety-aligned.

## Writing a constitution

The constitution is a list of principles the critique and judge stages apply. It is short by design — the point of CAI is that a small human artifact (a page of principles) governs a large machine artifact (millions of training rows). Length ranges from a handful of principles (~10) to a page or two (~30–50).

Anthropic's original CAI paper published its constitution — a mix of UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights excerpts, Apple / DeepMind terms-of-service language, and Anthropic-written principles targeting specific failure modes. Anthropic's later "Collective Constitutional AI" work (2023) crowdsourced principles from a public panel. Both are worth reading as examples; each is under 100 principles.

### Principle categories

A working constitution covers, roughly:

- **Harmlessness principles.** "Please choose the response that is least harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, dangerous, or illegal." One or two general principles + specific ones for your policy surface (CBRN, PII, self-harm, etc.).
- **Helpfulness principles.** "Please choose the response that is most helpful, honest, and direct." Without these, critique-and-revise degenerates to over-refusal — every revision adds hedges.
- **Honesty principles.** "Please choose the response that is more honest and does not deceive the reader, even if less pleasant." Necessary because purely-harmlessness-driven revisions can produce polite fabrications.
- **Voice / style principles.** "Please choose the response that is concise and does not moralize." Encodes the response style from chapter 2.
- **Domain-specific principles** if your policy surface has them. "Please choose the response that does not provide legal advice as if from a lawyer." Only add these when general principles do not cover the case.

### Principle template

Each principle is worded to be usable in *both* the critique and the judge prompt. The Anthropic-style template — a natural-language directive plus a pointer to the response being evaluated — reads:

```
Please choose the response that <property clause>.

For example, if the responses discuss weapons, please choose the one
that <specific guidance>.
```

Concrete example (paraphrased from Anthropic's published constitution):

> Please choose the response that is least likely to be harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, toxic, dangerous, or illegal.

And a domain example you might add:

> Please choose the response that provides medical information only in general terms and always recommends consulting a licensed physician for specific diagnoses or treatment.

Principles are natural language. The critique / judge model interprets them the same way a human would. This is both the strength (no formal specification burden) and the weakness (interpretation is imperfect) of CAI.

### How many principles

The empirical guidance from the Anthropic paper: too many principles hurt. Their published constitution used a rotating single-principle-per-row scheme — each preference judgment applied one randomly-sampled principle, not the whole list — because injecting all principles at once made the judge less discerning.

Practically: start with 10–20 principles; run the critique loop; hand-audit ~100 revisions; adjust the principles that produce weird outputs; re-run.

## Implementing the critique-and-revise loop

Concretely, stage 1 (SL-CAI) is a two-model pipeline. You need:

- **A generator model** — the base or SFT model whose responses you want to improve. Small (7B) is fine; the exercise runs at this scale.
- **A critic / reviser model** — the model that produces the critique and the revision. In principle this can be the same as the generator (self-critique) or stronger (distilling from a bigger model). The original CAI paper used self-critique with the same model. Modern practice often uses a stronger model as the critic to get sharper revisions.
- **A prompt corpus** — the red-team prompts you want to elicit potentially-unsafe responses on. AdvBench, HarmBench, or a custom set from chapter 4's red-team ingest work here.

The loop, in code-sketch form:

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

generator = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("<generator_model>")
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("<generator_model>")
# critic could be same as generator or a stronger model
critic = generator  # self-critique variant

CONSTITUTION = [
    "Please choose the response that is least harmful, unethical, "
    "or dangerous.",
    "Please choose the response that is honest and does not deceive "
    "the reader.",
    "Please choose the response that is helpful and does not refuse "
    "unnecessarily.",
    # ... 10–20 total
]

def critique_and_revise(prompt: str) -> dict:
    # 1. Initial response from generator
    y0 = generate(generator, prompt)

    # 2. Sample a principle (or a few)
    principle = random.choice(CONSTITUTION)

    # 3. Critique
    critique_prompt = f"""
Consider the following prompt and response.

Prompt: {prompt}
Response: {y0}

{principle}
Identify specific ways the response fails this criterion.
If it already meets the criterion, say so.
""".strip()
    c = generate(critic, critique_prompt)

    # 4. Revise
    revise_prompt = f"""
Given the prompt, the initial response, and the critique below,
write a revised response that addresses the critique while still
being genuinely helpful when the request is legitimate.

Prompt: {prompt}
Initial response: {y0}
Critique: {c}
Revised response:
""".strip()
    y1 = generate(critic, revise_prompt)

    return {
        "prompt": prompt,
        "initial_response": y0,
        "principle": principle,
        "critique": c,
        "revised_response": y1,
    }
```

A few implementation notes that the sketch elides:

- **Chat template.** Use the *actual* chat template of both models. Bare-string concatenation like the sketch works for smoke tests; real runs use `tokenizer.apply_chat_template` (mod-101 chapter on chat templates).
- **Batching.** Critique-and-revise is expensive — three generations per input row. Batch aggressively; use vLLM or TGI for a real corpus.
- **Sampling.** Use `temperature ≈ 0.7` for the initial response (you want the model's real distribution); lower for the critique and revision (`0.2–0.4`) so you get more consistent judgments and rewrites.
- **Multi-pass revision.** The original paper cycles critique-and-revise 3–4 times, sampling a new principle each time. Later revisions catch failures earlier ones missed. Each round costs compute; two rounds is a reasonable default.
- **Filter out no-op revisions.** Many revisions will be "the response already meets the criterion" — no change. Drop these rows from the SFT set; they add no signal. Alternatively, use them as *validation* that your generator is already safe on that prompt.

The outputs — the `(prompt, revised_response)` rows — are the SL-CAI SFT training set. Blend into your general SFT mix like any other safe-completion rows (chapter 2's SFT-side mix). Ratio guidance is the same.

## The AI-judge stage (RL-CAI)

Stage 2 replaces the critique-and-revise with a *comparison*.

```python
def judge_pair(prompt, y_a, y_b) -> dict:
    principle = random.choice(CONSTITUTION)
    judge_prompt = f"""
Consider the following prompt and two candidate responses.

Prompt: {prompt}
Response A: {y_a}
Response B: {y_b}

{principle}
Which response better meets this criterion? Answer only "A" or "B".
""".strip()
    ans = generate(judge, judge_prompt, max_new_tokens=1)
    winner = "A" if "A" in ans else "B"
    return {
        "prompt": prompt,
        "chosen": y_a if winner == "A" else y_b,
        "rejected": y_b if winner == "A" else y_a,
        "principle": principle,
    }
```

Sample `y_a` and `y_b` by running the *SL-CAI model* twice per prompt at higher temperature, or once from the SL-CAI model and once from the base. Both are documented in the CAI paper. The former produces sharper "which of two safe-ish responses is safer" pairs; the latter produces stronger "the safe version wins over the unsafe version" pairs.

Judge notes:

- **The A/B position is a bias source.** Judges have documented preferences for A over B (or vice versa) independent of content. Randomize order across rows; report per-position agreement in your dataset card.
- **Chain-of-thought judgments.** For harder cases, ask for a short justification before the final letter — "First explain in one or two sentences which response is better. Then answer with only 'A' or 'B'." The justification is usually worth logging even when you throw it away for training.
- **Judge model choice.** A strong general model (Claude, GPT-4, or a specialist open judge like `PairRM`, `Prometheus-2`, or `LLM-Blender`) gets you better labels than the model being trained. Anthropic's original paper used the same model as both generator and judge (a "self-alignment" variant); the follow-up literature has broadly moved toward using a stronger external judge.

The output — `(prompt, chosen, rejected, principle)` rows — is a safety-preference dataset, consumed by `DPOTrainer` / `KTOTrainer` (mod-105). Blend into your general preference set per chapter 2's mix guidance.

## Compute cost, honestly

CAI is not free. Rough compute breakdown for a 5000-prompt SL-CAI + RL-CAI run:

- **SL-CAI:** 5000 prompts × (1 initial + 1 critique + 1 revision) × ~2 rounds = ~30,000 generations. Even at 100 tokens each, at 100 tok/s on a single GPU, that's roughly 8 hours of generation time before any training.
- **RL-CAI:** 5000 prompts × 2 candidates + 5000 judge calls = 15,000 generations. Another few hours.
- **Training:** the SFT + DPO passes themselves are the same as mod-103 / mod-105; add their compute.

The reason CAI is popular anyway is that the *human* input is roughly constant regardless of dataset size — you write the constitution once. When the dataset needs to be regenerated (policy change, new model version, new red-team pass), you re-run the pipeline; you do not re-hire labelers.

For an exercise, use a small model (Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.2-3B) and a small prompt set (500–1000 prompts). The pipeline is identical to the production one; only the scale differs.

## When RLAIF is better than plain preference data — and when it isn't

CAI / RLAIF is one of three ways to produce a safety-preference set:

1. **Hand-labeled by humans.** Highest per-label quality; expensive; slow; harder to iterate.
2. **Existing open corpora** (chapter 2's list). Cheapest; you inherit whatever policies the source used; limited to the coverage the source has.
3. **Constitutional / RLAIF.** Costly in compute but cheap in human time; policy-flexible; scales to whatever prompt coverage you can generate.

The rough decision table:

| Situation | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Your policy differs materially from published open corpora (e.g., domain-specific customer-support surface, novel language, non-Western cultural context) | **CAI / RLAIF** — the constitution captures your policy; humans would need extensive per-task guidance |
| You already have 100k+ hand-labeled safety pairs and no strong reason to add more | **Human-labeled** — you have the data; don't re-do it |
| You are running a single first safety pass and want to move quickly | **Existing corpora** (chapter 2's mix), maybe supplemented with a small CAI run for gap categories |
| You need to update your safety training every few weeks to keep up with red-team findings | **CAI / RLAIF** — the pipeline is re-runnable |
| Your target model is small (<3B) and CAI compute is a significant fraction of training compute | **Existing corpora** — the CAI-generated data on top of a small budget may not earn its cost |
| You have a strong LLM available as a judge and are training a smaller student model | **RLAIF** — this is the exact configuration RLAIF was measured to work best on (Lee et al., 2023) |

Lee et al.'s RLAIF paper measured — on summarization and helpful-dialogue tasks — that AI-labeled preferences produced reward models roughly comparable to human-labeled ones, at a fraction of the cost. On safety specifically, they found parity to slight advantage for AI-labeling when the judge was a strong instruction-tuned model. Anthropic's own CAI paper reported that the CAI-trained model was preferred over the RLHF-trained baseline on harmlessness metrics without significantly sacrificing helpfulness. Neither paper claims RLAIF is universally better — the finding is that it is *competitive*, at much lower cost.

## Failure modes specific to CAI / RLAIF

The mod-105 preference-optimization failures (reward hacking, length bias, KL collapse, reference drift) all still apply. CAI adds its own:

### Judge misalignment

The judge model has its *own* preferences (length bias, format bias, sycophancy) that leak into your preference labels. If the judge over-values verbose responses, your DPO run will train verbosity. If the judge prefers responses that agree with the user, you get sycophancy.

**Mitigation:** cross-judge sample audits. Take 200 pairs the judge labeled; re-label with a *different* judge (a different family, ideally). Agreement below ~80% means the judge is producing idiosyncratic labels; either switch judges or hand-audit the disagreements.

### Constitutional monoculture

If every training row was generated / judged against the same constitution, your model over-fits to that constitution's specific interpretations. Novel prompts that hit a gap in the principles get inconsistent treatment.

**Mitigation:** review the constitution at every training iteration. Add principles for categories where the trained model produces surprises. Rotate the sampled principle per row so no single principle dominates.

### Distillation collapse

If your critic / judge is a much stronger model, CAI is really a *distillation* pipeline — you are teaching the student model to imitate the teacher's safety judgments. The student can only be as safe as the teacher on the covered prompts.

**Mitigation:** be honest about what you are doing. In a distillation setup, disclose the teacher model in the dataset card (some model licenses restrict this). Consider whether direct fine-tuning of the teacher would be a simpler pipeline than distillation-through-CAI.

### Feedback loop amplification

If you use the *trained* model as the next iteration's critic, any bias in the trained model becomes the training signal for the next iteration. Amplifies rapidly.

**Mitigation:** in iterative CAI, keep the judge fixed across iterations (or replace it with an independently-tuned judge on a fresh schedule), and always retain human-labeled eval sets for out-of-loop measurement.

### Cost overhang and stopping criterion

CAI runs can be extended indefinitely — generate more prompts, generate more revisions, generate more pairs. There is no natural stopping point. Runs sometimes over-generate data without corresponding gains on the eval dashboard.

**Mitigation:** define the stopping criterion up front. "Run until refusal-rate-on-HarmBench > X% and false-refusal-rate-on-XSTest < Y%, then stop." Instrument the eval dashboard from step 1.

## Related self-critique techniques

Two adjacent techniques worth knowing by name; both are variations on the CAI theme.

**Self-Instruct** (Wang et al., 2022) — the *instruction generation* half of the same pattern. A model generates new instruction-following examples from a seed set, and a filter (rule-based or model-based) keeps the good ones. Not safety-specific but shares the "model produces its own training data" shape. The mod-102 chapter on synthetic data covered this.

**SELF-ALIGN / Dromedary** (Sun et al., 2023) — a bootstrapping variant that combines Self-Instruct with a Constitutional-AI-style principle set to generate instructions *and* their aligned responses from scratch, using minimal human data (16 hand-written principles + 195 seed prompts in the paper). Useful pointer if you want the "with almost no labeled data" recipe.

**Self-refine / Reflexion** (Madaan et al., 2023 and Shinn et al., 2023) — inference-time critique-and-revise loops applied to task performance (not safety training). Same shape at test time rather than training time; the safety application is essentially Constitutional AI applied online.

**RLAIF-V** (Yu et al., 2024) — vision-language variant. If your model is multimodal, the same CAI pattern extends to image-and-text inputs, with visual harm principles added to the constitution.

## Summary

- Constitutional AI replaces human safety-labeling with an LLM applying a **written constitution** of principles. The team writes the constitution once; the model applies it at scale.
- Two-stage recipe: **SL-CAI** (critique-and-revise produces safer SFT rows) and **RL-CAI / RLAIF** (AI-judge produces safety-preference pairs). Either can be run alone; both together is the full Anthropic recipe.
- Constitutions are short (10–50 principles) and cover harmlessness, helpfulness, honesty, voice, and any domain-specific policy. Rotate the sampled principle per row to keep judgments from monoculturing.
- Implement the loop with three functions — generate initial response, critique against a principle, revise — using an open generator and a stronger critic. Batch aggressively; multi-pass; filter no-op revisions.
- CAI is not free (30k+ generations per 5k-prompt run) but scales linearly in compute rather than in human labor. Use it when your policy differs from open corpora, when you need iteration cadence, or when you have a strong judge available.
- CAI-specific failures: **judge misalignment**, **constitutional monoculture**, **distillation collapse**, **feedback loop amplification**, **no natural stopping criterion**. Instrument the eval dashboard from step 1; audit judge labels cross-model; hold humans in the loop for out-of-loop measurement.
- Adjacent techniques worth naming: Self-Instruct (data generation), SELF-ALIGN / Dromedary (bootstrapping), Self-Refine / Reflexion (inference-time), RLAIF-V (multimodal).
