# Refusal and Harm-Reduction Datasets

**Estimated read:** 45 minutes.
**You should be able to, by the end:** name the open corpora that a first safety-tuning run pulls from, describe the SFT-safe vs preference-safe split and pick a mix ratio for each, catalog the response-style choices your training set encodes (hard refusal vs soft refusal vs safe completion), and describe the specific quality controls that keep a safety set from becoming an over-refusal generator.

## The two shapes of safety training data

Safety data comes in the same two shapes that mod-102 and mod-105 introduced, and it is routed into the same two trainers:

- **Safe completion SFT rows** — `(prompt, response)`. Consumed by the `SFTTrainer` from mod-103. The `response` is the safe behavior you want: a refusal, a reframed answer, a redacted output, a warning-prefixed answer.
- **Safety preference pairs** — `(prompt, chosen_safer, rejected_less_safe)`. Consumed by `DPOTrainer` / `KTOTrainer` / `ORPOTrainer` from mod-105. The `chosen` is the safer response for that prompt; the `rejected` is a less-safe response *for the same prompt* — often the base model's own unfiltered output, sometimes a hand-crafted or model-generated bad response.

Both shapes are needed. SFT rows *teach the response shape* — "this is what a well-formed refusal looks like in your voice, at your length, with your policy citations." Preference pairs *teach the discrimination* — "given a prompt where the model *could* have said either of these two things, the safer one is preferred." Trying to do either job with only the other shape leaves gaps.

The rest of this chapter is about where to source both shapes, how much of each to mix in, and how to keep the mix from teaching the wrong lesson.

## Open corpora to know

You will almost never start a safety-tuning run from scratch. The open ecosystem publishes several corpora that provide the base signal. Learn the top-level structure of these before you commit; each has a bias you inherit.

### Anthropic HH-RLHF — helpful & harmless

Bai et al., 2022 ("Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback"). ~170k conversation-level preference pairs, split into `helpful-base`, `helpful-online`, `helpful-rejection-sampled`, and `harmless-base`. The `harmless-base` split is the one directly relevant here: multi-turn conversations where the assistant is being pushed toward or away from harmful content, and annotators picked the "more harmless" final assistant turn.

- **Strengths:** The oldest and largest open safety-preference corpus; well-studied; downstream reproduction (Zephyr, Tulu, the alignment handbook) uses it as a reference. Multi-turn conversations exercise the same shape that production chat sees.
- **Weaknesses:** The responses were generated by Anthropic's 2021–2022 base models; response quality is dated. Annotator disagreement is nontrivial on the margins (Bai et al. report annotator agreement in the low-to-mid 70s%). Some of the "harmless" preferences favor lecturing / over-hedged responses that would now be considered over-refusal.
- **License:** MIT (dataset code); dataset itself CC-BY-4.0. Widely used commercially.
- **Hub path:** `Anthropic/hh-rlhf`.

### PKU-SafeRLHF — safety-focused preference pairs

Ji et al., 2023 ("BeaverTails: Towards Improved Safety Alignment of LLM via a Human-Preference Dataset") and follow-ups. Two closely related releases: **BeaverTails** (QA-format prompts with per-response safety labels *and* helpfulness labels, plus 14-category harm tags) and **PKU-SafeRLHF** (preference pairs derived from BeaverTails).

- **Strengths:** Purpose-built for safety training. Dual annotation (safety + helpfulness) lets you build a chosen-based-on-safety vs chosen-based-on-helpfulness comparison, which is exactly the trade-off from chapter 1. Category tags let you produce coverage tables in your dataset card.
- **Weaknesses:** Prompts have a distinctive academic phrasing that does not always match production chat distribution. Safety labels are policy-specific to the annotators.
- **Hub paths:** `PKU-Alignment/PKU-SafeRLHF`, `PKU-Alignment/BeaverTails`.
- **License:** CC-BY-NC-4.0 (non-commercial) — check before shipping to production.

### Do-Not-Answer

Wang et al., 2023 ("Do-Not-Answer: A Dataset for Evaluating Safeguards in LLMs"). Around 939 prompts covering five risk areas and 12 harm subtypes, each with a "should be refused" label. Originally an *evaluation* set — designed to test whether a model correctly refuses — but the prompts are also usable as training prompts if you generate refusal responses for them.

- **Strengths:** Short, well-taxonomized, easy to add coverage on categories you find under-served.
- **Weaknesses:** Small; on its own it is not enough for training, only augmentation.
- **Hub path:** `LibrAI/do-not-answer`.

### SafetyBench, Aegis, HarmfulQA

Several smaller, category-tagged corpora sit alongside the above and are worth naming.

- **SafetyBench** (Zhang et al., 2024) — multiple-choice safety benchmark; useful for evaluation more than training.
- **NVIDIA Aegis AI Content Safety Dataset** — content-moderation labeled data used to train NVIDIA's Aegis classifier. Category taxonomy overlaps with Llama Guard's.
- **HarmfulQA** (Bhardwaj & Poria, 2023) — 1960 harmful questions across 10 topics with a chain-of-thought jailbreak dataset for training red-team-style attacks.

None of these is a stand-alone training set; each is best treated as a supplement that lets you cover a category the primary source under-represents.

### Attack corpora (prompts only, no responses)

These are prompt sets — often used as *inputs* into your own response-generation pipeline (chapter 3's CAI loop, chapter 4's red-team ingest). No labeled responses; you generate the responses yourself.

- **AdvBench** (Zou et al., 2023, "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models") — 520 harmful behaviors + 500 harmful strings. The de-facto attack-prompt set for jailbreak research.
- **HarmBench** (Mazeika et al., 2024) — 400 unique behaviors covering seven semantic categories (cybercrime, chemical/biological, illegal activities, misinformation, harassment, harmful, copyright). Ships with a standardized evaluation protocol.
- **JailbreakBench** (Chao et al., 2024) — 100 harmful and 100 benign behaviors with reference responses; ships with a leaderboard.
- **Llama Guard's training taxonomy prompts** — Inan et al. (2023, "Llama Guard: LLM-based Input-Output Safeguard for Human-AI Conversations") describe the schema and category prompts used to train the classifier; you can reuse the taxonomy to structure your own prompt coverage.
- **WildGuardMix** (Han et al., 2024, "WildGuard: Open One-Stop Moderation Tools for Safety Risks, Jailbreaks, and Refusals of LLMs") — 92k examples across in-the-wild prompts, adversarial jailbreaks, and vanilla safety scenarios, labeled for prompt harmfulness, response harmfulness, and refusal.

### Over-refusal / benign-adversarial sets

The other half of the balance: sets of prompts that *look* dangerous but should be answered. Included in your training only cautiously (see later section on over-refusal control), but you *always* keep them for evaluation.

- **XSTest** (Röttger et al., 2023) — 250 safe prompts and 200 unsafe prompts; the classical over-refusal test suite.
- **OR-Bench** (Cui et al., 2024, "OR-Bench: An Over-Refusal Benchmark for Large Language Models") — 80k over-refusal test prompts across ten categories.
- **PHTest** — a smaller pseudo-harmful test suite that pairs matched safe/unsafe surface features.

## The response-style choices your data encodes

Every row in your training set encodes an implicit answer to two questions: **what class of response is right for this prompt** and **what does the surface form of that response look like in our voice**. Both are decisions you should make consciously.

### Response class (from chapter 1, expanded)

| Class | When | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard refusal** | Any assistance is harmful (CBRN weapon synthesis, CSAM, non-consensual sexual content, targeted harassment against a named individual) | "I can't help with that." |
| **Soft refusal with reframing** | The literal ask is out-of-policy but adjacent legitimate uses exist | "I can't provide instructions for [X], but I can explain the [chemistry / history / legal context] of [X] if that would help." |
| **Safe completion** | The ask can be answered without the harmful part; the harmful part is removable | Redacts PII from the answer; provides crisis-line resources alongside a distress signal; strips a targeted-insult framing from an otherwise-general answer |
| **Full answer** | Prompt is in-policy despite surface features | Full answer with no refusal preamble |

**The classifier your dataset trains is exactly the classifier that decides which of these four your model produces per prompt.** If your training rows are 80% hard refusal, your model will hard-refuse 80% of the time on any prompt that pattern-matches to your training refusals. If your rows are stylistically inconsistent — some warm, some clinical, some lecture-y, some terse — your model produces stylistically inconsistent refusals. If your safe-completion rows use one specific format for "redacting PII" (bracket, ellipsis, `[REDACTED]`), that's the format that ships.

Design the target distribution across these four classes deliberately. A rough starting split for a general-purpose chat assistant, sanity-checked against public safety-tuning practice (Anthropic's system card notes, Meta's Llama 3 model card, and public Zephyr / Tulu safety splits):

- ~40–60% full-answer rows on borderline / benign-adversarial prompts (this is your over-refusal control, mixed *into* the safety split, not just held out for eval)
- ~20–30% safe-completion rows (redaction, warning-prefixed, reframed)
- ~10–20% soft refusals with alternatives
- ~5–15% hard refusals

The *precise* numbers depend on your policy surface — a legal-advice tool might have a much higher hard-refusal share; a creative-writing tool much lower. What matters is that you have decided and can defend the shape.

### Surface style — the voice of a refusal

A refusal is a piece of natural-language writing. It has a length, a register, a set of stock phrases. Your training set fixes all of these. Watch out for:

- **Length.** A three-paragraph refusal is a common failure mode when the training set is dominated by early Anthropic-style responses that lectured on why the ask was harmful. Modern practice (per Meta and Anthropic's more recent public writing) prefers short, non-preachy refusals: one to three sentences.
- **Preambles.** "As an AI language model, I..." and "I understand you're interested in ..., but ..." are stock phrases that trained models will fixate on. Both are visible as fingerprints in a base-model comparison. Choose whether you want them in your voice and be consistent.
- **Justification-vs-not.** Some refusals include *why* ("This would provide detailed instructions that could be used to harm people"); some do not ("I can't help with that"). Both are defensible; mixed styles in a training set produce a model that inconsistently justifies. Pick one per class and stick with it.
- **Alternatives / reframes.** A refusal that offers an alternative ("but I can help with a related question about X") is *safer to over-train* than a bare refusal — it gives the model a way to preserve helpfulness even when it errs on the side of caution. Prefer alternative-including refusals as the default.
- **Language.** Multilingual policy surfaces need multilingual refusals — non-English refusals often shift back to English mid-response when the training data is English-only, which is both a UX bug and a jailbreak surface.

## The safety mix — how much and where

Two mix ratios matter and they interact.

### SFT-side mix

Add safe-completion rows to the general SFT mix (mod-102 chapter on data mixes). The typical range is **2–10% of SFT tokens**. Concrete data points:

- Anthropic's public writing on HH-RLHF training (Bai et al., 2022) reports the harmless split as a nontrivial minority of the RLHF phase but does not publish a precise ratio.
- Meta's Llama 3 model card describes safety training as a mix of adversarial (attack-simulating) and borderline prompts alongside standard SFT data, at a ratio the paper leaves qualitative rather than numeric.
- Public reproductions like Tulu-3 (Ai2, 2024) publish their exact mix — a few tens of thousands of safety-focused SFT rows blended into a several-hundred-thousand-row instruction set — putting them at low single-digit percent by row count.

**Under 2%:** safety behavior is fragile; the model reverts to the base model's refusal (or lack thereof) on paraphrased attacks. **Over 10%:** the model starts over-refusing benign prompts that share surface features with the safety rows. Both are measurable — chapter 5's XSTest / OR-Bench + HarmBench dashboard is what tells you if you are outside the sweet spot.

### Preference-side mix

Add safety-preference pairs to the DPO / KTO training set. Typical range: **5–25% of preference pairs**, higher when the policy surface is complex or when a red-team pass has generated a lot of pair-material (chapter 4).

The preference-side mix is where you get the most bang for the buck. A well-curated 5000-pair safety-preference set on top of a general 30k UltraFeedback-style preference set will move refusal-rate-on-harmful substantially without touching refusal-rate-on-benign much — provided the safety pairs share the general set's overall response length and style. When the safety pairs are systematically shorter (bare refusals) than the general pairs, DPO can accidentally teach the model that "short response = safer response = preferred," and short-response bias leaks into helpfulness answers. This is the length-bias failure from mod-105 chapter 7, showing up in a safety-specific costume.

### Interaction between the two

A common recipe order:

1. **SFT with a safe-completion mix.** Establishes the *shape* of your safety responses. Model can now produce a well-formed refusal when it decides to refuse.
2. **DPO / KTO with a safety-preference mix on top of the general preference set.** Teaches the model *when* to refuse — the discrimination between "this prompt warrants a refusal" and "this prompt warrants a full answer."
3. **(Optional) A safety-only DPO pass.** If the previous DPO run did not move refusal-rate-on-harmful enough, run a second DPO stage on *only* the safety pairs, at a small learning rate and one epoch. This is called a "targeted safety pass" in practice; over-doing it is the fastest route to over-refusal.

Every stage measurable with the chapter 5 dashboard. Every stage reversible by rolling back to the previous checkpoint if the eval goes the wrong way.

## The over-refusal control problem

Everything above is the easy half of the module. The hard half is *not* over-refusing.

The mechanism of over-refusal is worth stating cleanly. When you add safety training rows to your SFT or DPO mix, the model learns a discriminator: "prompts with feature X get a refusal response." The features it latches onto are whatever surface signals reliably predict the refusal label in your training set — words ("kill," "weapon," "hack," "drugs"), phrasings ("how do I make," "step by step"), structural cues (very short prompts, imperative mood, mentions of specific chemicals). Some of those signals are genuinely correlated with harmful intent; some are not. The model does not know the difference; it just knows what your training set said.

Result: any benign prompt that hits the same surface features gets a refusal. "How do I kill a Python process," "how do I make a bomb-shaped cake for a birthday party," "how do I clean my shotgun for the safe I'm selling" — a naively-trained safety model refuses all of these. XSTest was built precisely to expose this class of failure.

The counter-training has three components. All three go into a real safety dataset.

### Component 1: Benign-adversarial exemplars in the training set

Include prompts that share surface features with your refusal set but should be *answered*. Each is labeled with a full answer, not a refusal.

Sources:

- **XSTest safe split** as *examples* (not test-set contamination — see caveat below).
- **OR-Bench** has a large enough set (80k) that you can safely use a training subset and hold out a distinct eval subset. Verify no overlap.
- **Hand-generated benign parallels of your refusal prompts.** If you have a refusal training row for "how do I kill someone," generate a parallel training row for "how do I kill this stubborn Python process." The lexical overlap is what teaches the model to distinguish.
- **WildGuardMix's benign / non-harmful split.**

A common ratio: **for every refusal training row on a "keyword-triggered" prompt, include 1–3 full-answer rows on benign parallels with the same keyword.** The parallels are the disambiguation signal that keeps your refusal discriminator honest.

**Contamination caveat:** if XSTest is in your training set, XSTest is no longer a valid eval. Chapter 5 goes into eval hygiene; the short version is *hold out at least one benign-adversarial benchmark from training entirely*, and preferably split any set you use for training into disjoint train / eval halves and document the split hashes in the dataset card.

### Component 2: Full-answer alternatives inside soft refusals

When you *do* refuse, prefer refusals that suggest an alternative direction. "I can't help you [X], but I can [Y]" is safer to over-generalize on than "I can't help with that." Downstream, if your model over-refuses on a benign prompt, an alternative-suggesting response is *still useful*; a bare refusal is not.

This is a stylistic choice encoded in the training data. Every refusal row in your SFT set should have a fixed decision: does it include an alternative or not? Model that decision by class (hard refusals: no alternative; soft refusals: always alternative).

### Component 3: Explicit test-and-fix loop

The most important control is measurement. After each safety-training run:

1. Score refusal rate on a harmful test set (HarmBench, JailbreakBench harmful behaviors).
2. Score false-refusal rate on a benign-adversarial test set (XSTest safe split, OR-Bench).
3. If either regressed, iterate.

Chapter 5 is the full playbook. The dataset engineering half — this chapter — is only judged by what the numbers say in chapter 5.

## Category coverage

Your policy surface has categories. Your dataset must cover them proportionally. Underserved categories are exactly where post-deployment red teams and users find failures.

The concrete pattern: **build a coverage table** in your dataset card. Rows are your taxonomy categories (chapter 1's Weidinger / Anthropic / AILuminate / your own). Columns are `refusal_train_count`, `benign_parallel_train_count`, `preference_pair_count`, `eval_prompt_count`. Every non-zero row in your taxonomy needs a non-zero count in every column, and the ratios should roughly match your policy priorities.

For a general-purpose chat assistant, an example coverage table (illustrative — your numbers will vary):

| Category | Refusal SFT | Benign parallels | Preference pairs | Eval prompts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Violent crime | 500 | 800 | 400 | 50 |
| CBRN | 400 | 200 | 500 | 50 |
| Self-harm | 200 | 400 | 300 | 40 |
| Sexual content | 300 | 500 | 400 | 40 |
| Hate speech | 400 | 600 | 400 | 50 |
| Privacy / PII | 300 | 500 | 300 | 40 |
| Specialized advice (med/legal/fin) | 400 | 1000 | 500 | 50 |
| Cybercrime | 400 | 600 | 400 | 50 |
| Manipulation / deception | 300 | 400 | 300 | 30 |
| Copyright / IP | 200 | 400 | 200 | 30 |
| **Total** | **3400** | **5400** | **3700** | **430** |

Two things to notice:

- **Benign parallels are usually the largest column.** They have to be, to offset the surface-feature discriminator.
- **The eval count per category is small.** 30–50 prompts per category is enough for meaningful rates as long as your CI'd numbers are honest about the interval. Chapter 5 covers reporting.

## Dataset-card checklist

Ship a dataset card with every safety training set. On top of the general preference-dataset card (mod-105 chapter 2), add these safety-specific sections:

- **Taxonomy in use.** Which categories you chose to cover; the source (Weidinger / Anthropic / AILuminate / custom).
- **Category coverage table.** Row counts per category × response class.
- **Response-style choices.** For each category × response class, a canonical example.
- **Benign-parallel provenance.** Where the benign-adversarial prompts came from; the hashing / dedup approach used to keep them out of the eval sets.
- **Over-refusal audit.** Score of the *dataset itself* on an XSTest-like sample: if you take 50 benign-adversarial prompts and check whether *your training data* would have generated a refusal (this is a hand-inspection or LLM-judge check on the closest-neighbor rows in your dataset), what's the false-positive rate? A high number here predicts a high over-refusal rate in the trained model.
- **License chain.** Every source dataset's license. Some safety sets (BeaverTails, PKU-SafeRLHF) are non-commercial; a mixed set inherits the most-restrictive license unless you have re-licensed the derived rows.
- **Eval decontamination.** Which safety eval sets (XSTest, HarmBench, JailbreakBench, OR-Bench) you checked prompt overlap against, and the overlap counts.

Exercise 01 has you build this card for a real dataset.

## Common recipes

Two starting configurations that work well as first cuts.

**Recipe A: general-purpose chat, minimal risk surface.** Blend UltraFeedback-Binarized (the general preference set from mod-105 exercise 01) with a 3000-pair PKU-SafeRLHF safety split at ~10% mix. Add 500 SFT refusal rows drawn from PKU-SafeRLHF's chosen responses. Add 500 benign parallels hand-generated by prompting a strong open model for benign completions of the same surface keywords. Train DPO one epoch; measure with chapter 5's dashboard.

**Recipe B: policy-surface-heavy assistant (customer service, medical / legal domain).** Start from Recipe A. Add 2000–5000 domain-specific refusal SFT rows (constitutionally generated per chapter 3 or written by a domain SME). Add 5000+ benign parallels covering the domain vocabulary. Split preference pairs 60/40 general/safety instead of 90/10. Consider a second targeted-safety DPO pass.

Both recipes should be *starting points* to iterate from, driven by the chapter 5 dashboard. Neither is a finished recipe.

## Common mistakes

Failures worth naming so you can avoid them:

1. **Training only on refusals.** No benign parallels; the model learns "refuse everything with these keywords." Over-refusal explodes.
2. **Single-style refusals.** Every refusal is the same three-paragraph template. The model produces that exact template even on prompts that warranted a one-sentence "no."
3. **Contamination.** XSTest is in the training set; XSTest score reads 95%; the model still over-refuses everywhere else.
4. **Length mismatch.** Safety refusals are all 10 tokens; general responses are all 400 tokens. DPO learns "short = safe = preferred"; general answers get shorter and lose helpfulness.
5. **Missing categories.** Coverage table has a zero row. Post-deployment red team hits that category; there is no training signal to correct against.
6. **Stale response style.** Refusals are copied verbatim from HH-RLHF's 2021-era style ("As a language model, I cannot..."). Ships to production; users complain about the robotic voice.
7. **License violation.** Uses BeaverTails (non-commercial) in a commercial product without re-licensing.
8. **No preference-side coverage.** Only SFT-side safety training. Model produces well-formed refusals but the discrimination between "should refuse" and "should answer" is weak.

## Summary

- Safety data comes in two shapes: **safe-completion SFT rows** (teach response shape) and **safety-preference pairs** (teach when to refuse vs answer). Both are usually needed.
- The primary open corpora: **Anthropic HH-RLHF**, **PKU-SafeRLHF / BeaverTails**, **Do-Not-Answer**, plus prompt-only attack corpora (AdvBench, HarmBench, JailbreakBench, WildGuardMix) that you generate responses against.
- Four response classes — **hard refusal**, **soft refusal with reframing**, **safe completion**, **full answer** — and your training set's mix across these classes determines what your model actually produces. Design the split explicitly; do not let it happen by accident.
- Mix ratios: **2–10% of SFT tokens** for safe-completion rows; **5–25% of preference pairs** for safety pairs. Both measurable via chapter 5's dashboard; adjust when the numbers say so.
- **Over-refusal is the second-most-common failure of a safety-tuning run.** Counter it with (a) benign-adversarial parallels in the training set, (b) alternative-suggesting soft refusals as the default class, (c) a mandatory measure-and-iterate loop with XSTest / OR-Bench in the eval bank.
- Ship a **coverage table** in the dataset card: taxonomy category × response class × count. Zero rows predict production failures.
- Common failure modes: refusal-only training, single-style templates, eval contamination, length mismatch across safety-vs-general pairs, missing categories, stale response style, license violations.
